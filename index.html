About

What I work on, and how I ended up doing it.

Most of what I build has to hold up while someone is using it. In a match, a loose connector or a servo firing a fraction late is enough to lose it.

I'm a student at the University of Toronto Schools (Class of 2031) working across robotics, embedded systems, electronics and design. Most of what I know came from building something, breaking it, and building it again.

In competition I'm lead builder and driver for VEX team 19109M. Outside that it's hackathons and my own hardware: Tappy, where an NFC tap approves what an AI agent is about to spend; SkySaver, a drone that finds what you describe to it; and NeoPark, a parking gate I built from the RFID logic up to the servo that lifts the arm.

Calenda is the one that isn't hardware — a planner that keeps schoolwork, my schedule and family commitments in one place. I started it because I wanted it and couldn't find it.

The part I like most is where the drawing meets the actual part: tolerances, revisions, and testing something until it stops surprising me.

Competitive robotics · 2025–present

VEX 19109M — UTS Circuit Breakers

I'm lead builder and driver for our V5RC Push Back season — drivetrain and scoring mechanisms, autonomous routines, driving, and the strategy work between matches. In season it's 20+ hours a week.

How the season ran
  1. 1 Design and build the drivetrain and scoring mechanisms for Push Back.
  2. 2 Build the autonomous routine and tune it against the real field.
  3. 3 Driver practice until the controls are muscle memory, including elimination matches.
  4. 4 Scout opponents, set match strategy, then rebuild what didn't hold up.

Every competition told us something that changed the next version of the robot. Working out a mechanical or control failure on the field, with the next match about to start, turned out to be a skill of its own.

  • VEX V5
  • Autonomous
  • Mechanical design
  • Drivetrain
  • Scouting

03

Hardware + AI · Ignition Hacks 2026

SkySaver

Describe what you're looking for in plain language and the drone finds it in its live camera feed and tells you where it is. It's aimed at environmental monitoring. I built the drone hardware and worked on the code behind it, and we've kept going on it past the hackathon.

How it works
  1. 1 You describe what you're looking for in ordinary language.
  2. 2 Computer vision scans the drone's live camera feed for a match.
  3. 3 GPS pins where the match actually is.
  4. 4 It gives you somewhere to go, not just a box drawn on a screen.

The hard part is the handoffs — turning a description into something the camera can recognise, then turning that into a place on the ground.

  • Drone hardware
  • Computer vision
  • GPS
  • Natural language
  • TypeScript

Built at Ignition Hacks, August 2026 — still in development

04

Hardware + AI · ETHOnline 2026

Tappy

An Ethereum wallet an AI agent can use. The agent works out the transaction, but nothing moves until you tap a cold NFC tag — or a Flipper Zero — to your phone. I built the NFC hardware side and worked on the implementation. The signing keys never sit on the agent.

How it works
  1. 1 The agent works out the transaction it wants to make on your behalf.
  2. 2 It can't sign anything — the keys never live on the agent.
  3. 3 You tap a cold NFC tag or a Flipper Zero to your phone. That tap is the approval.
  4. 4 Only then does the transaction go through.

Keeping the keys off the agent is the whole point. If it gets a decision wrong, it still has nothing to spend.

  • NFC
  • Flipper Zero
  • Hardware
  • AI agent
  • Ethereum

Team project, in progress — ETHOnline 2026

05

Embedded systems · 2025

NeoPark

My first hackathon project, built on my own. An Arduino parking gate: RFID checks the vehicle, an infrared sensor confirms it's actually there, a servo lifts the barrier and an LCD shows what it's doing.

How it works
  1. 1 An RFID reader scans the tag and checks it against the authorised IDs.
  2. 2 An infrared sensor confirms a vehicle is actually present at the gate.
  3. 3 Only with both true does the servo drive the barrier arm up.
  4. 4 The LCD reports the current state — reading, granted, denied, open.

Two things have to agree before anything moves: a valid tag on its own won't open an empty gate, and a car on its own can't force it.

  • Arduino
  • RFID
  • IR sensing
  • Servo control
  • 16x2 LCD

Best Science Fair Project — school-wide recognition
